## TOPIC 1 — Photoshop Can Now Relight Your Photos After You Shoot Them

### What Happened
At Adobe MAX 2026 (November 10), Adobe moved its AI "Relight" tool out of beta and into the main Photoshop release. Relight lets you add lights to a photo after it's taken and adjust their position, color and brightness. It runs on Adobe's generative AI and uses generative credits; Adobe's own community post says each generation costs 30 credits. Note: this is from Nov 10, so it's about 6 days old. Present it as "this just landed in Photoshop", not as breaking news.

### Why It Matters
Lighting is one of the hardest things to fix after a shoot. If AI can believably change the light afterward, it changes how photographers handle bad venue lighting, client retouching, and what clients expect. Creative Bloq reports it's already dividing opinion.

### Why It Suits Me
I shoot events where I don't control the lighting. I can talk about this as someone who deals with dark venues and mixed lighting at real gigs.

### Hook
"Photoshop can now move the light in your photo after you've already taken it, and I'm not sure how I feel about that."

### Talking Points
- Relight is now in the main Photoshop app, not just the beta. You can add lights and change their position, color and brightness after the shot.
- It's not free to use without limits: each generation uses 30 generative credits.
- It can rescue bad lighting, but it doesn't replace learning to light well on set.

### My Angle
As an event shooter, this is a safety net and not a skill. Clients pay for someone who gets it right in camera. Use Relight to save a shot, not as an excuse to shoot lazy. And honestly: if you edit everything with AI light, is it still your photo?

### 30–60 Second Script
Photoshop can now move the light in your photo after you've already taken it, and I'm not sure how I feel about that.

So Adobe just took a tool called Relight out of beta and put it in the main Photoshop. You can add lights to a photo after the shot and change where they come from, what color they are and how bright they are.

As someone who shoots events, I get the appeal. Half the venues I shoot in have terrible lighting. Being able to fix a face that's in shadow? That's huge.

But two things. First, it's not unlimited: Adobe says each generation uses 30 generative credits. Second, and this is the bigger one, clients hire you because you can get it right in camera. I see this as a safety net, not a replacement for actually learning light.

So what do you think? Is AI lighting still real photography, or is it cheating? Let me know below.

## TOPIC 2 — Photographer's Copyright Case Against Canadian Puzzle Maker Moves Forward

### What Happened
Landscape photographer Elliot McGucken says Canadian puzzle maker Karmin International put five of his photos on jigsaw puzzles without permission. The packaging reportedly said the images were licensed from Shutterstock. McGucken says Shutterstock told Karmin the licenses were invalid, but the puzzles kept selling. In early November, a US federal judge in New York (Judge John G. Koeltl, SDNY) allowed most of his claims to move forward. Reports date the ruling to November 3 or November 6. Coverage appeared on November 13–15. This is not a final verdict: the case can now proceed.

### Why It Matters
It shows that your photos can end up on products you never agreed to, even through "licensed" stock channels. It also shows that copyright notices, metadata and documentation matter when you need to enforce your rights.

### Why It Suits Me
I'm a photographer posting my work online every day, and the defendant is a Canadian company. Protecting your work is something every photographer and creator in my audience cares about.

### Hook
"A photographer found his pictures on jigsaw puzzles he never approved, and a judge just said he can take the company to court."

### Talking Points
- The puzzle maker allegedly used five of his landscape photos and claimed they were licensed through Shutterstock.
- He says Shutterstock flagged the licenses as invalid, but sales continued. The judge let most of his claims move forward.
- Lesson: keep your originals, keep your metadata, and know where your work is licensed.

### My Angle
Don't just say "protect your work". Make it practical: what I do as a working photographer, like keeping RAW files, keeping copyright info in the metadata, and running reverse image searches on my best shots. Also make clear the case isn't won yet. It's only allowed to go ahead.

### 30–60 Second Script
A photographer found his pictures on jigsaw puzzles he never approved, and a judge just said he can take the company to court.

His name is Elliot McGucken, a landscape photographer. He says a Canadian puzzle company, Karmin International, used five of his photos on puzzles. The box said the images were licensed through Shutterstock. But according to him, Shutterstock told the company those licenses were invalid, and the puzzles kept selling anyway.

A US federal judge has now allowed most of his claims to move forward. To be clear, he hasn't won yet. But the case is alive.

Here's why I'm telling you this. If you post photos online, your work can end up on stuff you never agreed to. So keep your RAW files, put your copyright info in your metadata, and every now and then, reverse image search your best shots.

Have you ever found your work somewhere you didn't expect? Tell me in the comments.

## TOPIC 3 — YouTube Studio Swaps "Analytics" for AI "Insights" and Adds a "Research" Tab

### What Happened
On November 13, YouTube updated YouTube Studio. On desktop, "Insights" now fronts the Analytics section and gives plain-language summaries of your channel's performance. Advanced analytics and real-time data reportedly haven't changed. A new "Research" tab replaces "Trends" and highlights "outlier" videos that perform above a channel's typical average. Ask Studio, YouTube's AI assistant, is now in the Studio mobile app and accepts up to 10 image uploads. YouTube says Ask Studio and image uploads are available to eligible creators globally.

### Why It Matters
Small creators often don't understand their analytics. Plain-language summaries and an outlier-video research tool make it easier to decide what to post next, especially for people posting Shorts.

### Why It Suits Me
I'm growing a small channel and learning to use AI tools in my workflow. I can test the new tabs on my own channel and react honestly as a small creator.

### Hook
"YouTube just changed the Analytics tab, and if you're a small creator, this might be the most useful update this year."

### Talking Points
- Analytics now leads with "Insights": AI-style summaries in plain English, on desktop. Your detailed numbers are still there.
- "Research" replaces "Trends" and shows outlier videos that are beating their channel's average, so you can spot ideas.
- Ask Studio is now on mobile and you can upload up to 10 images, for example screenshots of thumbnails or stats.

### My Angle
Test it live on my own small channel: open Research, find an outlier in photography or editing, and react. Opinion: AI summaries are helpful, but don't let them replace actually understanding your audience. Use Research for ideas, not to copy.

### 30–60 Second Script
YouTube just changed the Analytics tab, and if you're a small creator, this might be the most useful update this year.

So as of November 13th, when you open YouTube Studio on desktop, you'll see something called Insights. Basically, it explains how your channel is doing in plain English, instead of just throwing graphs at you. Your detailed analytics are still there.

The Trends tab is gone. It's been replaced with Research, which shows you outlier videos: videos doing way better than their channel normally does. That's basically a free idea generator.

And Ask Studio, YouTube's AI assistant, is now in the mobile app, and you can upload up to 10 images, like screenshots of your thumbnails.

My take? Use Research to find ideas, not to copy people. Go check your Studio and tell me what you see.

## TOPIC 1 — AI Is Cloning Creators' Viral Videos ("Reskinning")

### What Happened
A BBC Verify investigation (covered by The Next Web on Nov 23 and PetaPixel on Nov 25) found that musicians, dancers, comedians and other creators are discovering copies of their viral videos where they've been swapped out for an AI-generated character. The movements, background, timing and even jokes stay the same. It's called "reskinning," and cheap AI video tools make it easy. In some cases the copy got millions more views than the original.

### Why It Matters
If your face is swappable, your *performance* is what gets stolen — your idea, your timing, your location, your edit. Creators lose views, followers and potential income to accounts that did none of the work. Platforms are responding (Instagram expanded Content Protection on Nov 17; TikTok's Likeness Detection started rolling out globally Nov 19), but creators interviewed say new copycat accounts keep popping up.

### Why It Suits Me
I'm a creator and photographer who actually shoots original work — and I use AI tools myself. I can speak to both sides: AI as a tool vs. AI as a shortcut to steal someone else's creativity.

### Hook
"Someone can now take your viral video, swap you out for an AI character, and get more views than you did."

### Talking Points
- What "reskinning" is: same video, same moves, same background — just a new AI face.
- Real creators (like UK guitarist Sophie Lloyd) found AI copies of their performances, and some copies outperformed the originals.
- Platforms are adding tools (Instagram Content Protection, TikTok Likeness Detection), but creators say it's whack-a-mole.

### My Angle
AI isn't the villain — lazy copying is. I use AI in my workflow, but the value of a creator is the idea, the shot and the personality. The best protection right now: build a recognizable style and a real audience that knows *you*, post consistently so you're the clear original, and turn on whatever protection tools your platform gives you.

### 30–60 Second Script
Someone can now take your viral video, swap you out for an AI character, and get more views than you did.

It's called "reskinning." A BBC investigation found musicians, dancers and comedians whose videos were copied almost frame by frame — same moves, same background, same jokes — just with an AI person dropped in. One UK guitarist found AI versions of her performances being used by an account that the BBC couldn't find any evidence was even a real person.

And some of these copies got millions more views than the originals.

Instagram and TikTok are rolling out tools to help creators find copies of their content, but creators say every time one account gets taken down, another pops up.

Here's my take — I use AI tools. AI isn't the problem. Stealing someone's idea is. What can't be copied is *you* — your style, your voice, the people who actually follow you for you. So build that, post consistently, and turn on every protection tool your platform gives you.

Would you even notice if someone reskinned your video? Let me know.

## TOPIC 2 — A Photographer Took Deposits and Never Delivered — Now the State Is Investigating

### What Happened
Missouri's Attorney General opened an investigation into Leave It to Beaver Photography in Wentzville, Missouri, after customers said they paid for shoots and never got their photos or a refund. One bride paid $1,500 toward wedding and engagement coverage — the photographer didn't show up to the engagement party. Another paid $300 for family portraits and got nothing. The business reportedly closed after 13 years, according to its Facebook page, and local TV couldn't reach the owner for comment.

### Why It Matters
Every story like this makes clients more nervous about hiring photographers — especially for weddings and events, where you only get one shot. It's a reminder that contracts, clear delivery timelines and communication aren't "extra"; they're what separates a professional from a risk.

### Why It Suits Me
I run Pixel With Bliss and do event coverage. Talking about how clients can protect themselves (and how I handle delivery) builds trust with the exact people I want to book.

### Hook
"People paid a photographer for their wedding photos — and the state attorney general is now investigating because they never got them."

### Talking Points
- What happened: deposits taken, photos never delivered, no refunds, state investigation opened.
- Red flags clients can watch for: no written contract, no delivery date, slow or no replies.
- What a professional should give you: a contract, a delivery timeline, and updates along the way.

### My Angle
Flip it into a trust/education piece: "Here's what you should expect from any photographer you hire — including me." Not dunking on the photographer (the investigation is ongoing and nothing's been proven in court), but using it as a reason to raise standards. Soft CTA for Pixel With Bliss without being salesy.

### 30–60 Second Script
People paid a photographer for their wedding photos — and now a state attorney general is investigating because they never got them.

This is in Missouri. Customers say they paid a local photography business for weddings, family photos and maternity shoots — and never got their pictures or a refund. One bride paid fifteen hundred dollars, and the photographer didn't even show up to her engagement party. The business has reportedly shut down, and the investigation is still ongoing.

As a photographer, this honestly frustrates me, because it makes people scared to hire any of us.

So if you're booking a photographer for anything important, here are three things to ask for. One: a written contract. Two: a clear delivery date — in writing. Three: a photographer who actually replies to you before the event, because that's how they'll treat you after.

If someone can't give you those three things, keep looking. Your memories are worth more than a cheap deposit.

## TOPIC 3 — The Sony FX5 Is Now Netflix Approved

### What Happened
Netflix added the Sony FX5 to its approved camera list (reported Nov 25, 2026), along with a production guide. Netflix recommends shooting internal 16-bit X-OCN LT RAW in Cine EI at base ISO 800 with Dual Gain, with XAVC S-I 4K as the non-RAW option. The catch: Super 35 3.2K mode isn't recommended for primary capture, and Long-GOP/4:2:0 modes aren't recommended either. The FX5 was announced July 22, 2026 at US$4,899.99 (CA$6,299.99) body only.

### Why It Matters
A sub-$5K (USD) compact cinema camera is now cleared for Netflix originals. That shrinks the gap between indie/solo shooters and "real" productions. But "Netflix approved" depends on *how* you shoot it — the settings matter as much as the camera.

### Why It Suits Me
Cinematic film is my focus. Explaining what "Netflix approved" actually means — and what it doesn't — is a gear topic I can speak to as a shooter, not just a reviewer.

### Hook
"Sony's FX5 just got Netflix approved — but there's a catch most people are going to miss."

### Talking Points
- Netflix approved the FX5 and published recommended settings: X-OCN LT RAW, ISO 800, Dual Gain.
- The catch: the Super 35 crop mode doesn't qualify, and compressed Long-GOP/4:2:0 modes aren't recommended.
- "Netflix approved" doesn't make your footage cinematic — lighting, story and color grading still do.

### My Angle
Gear doesn't make you Netflix-level, but it's cool that the barrier keeps dropping. Most of us aren't shooting Netflix shows — but knowing *why* Netflix picks certain settings (RAW, proper base ISO, avoiding heavy compression) teaches you how to get cleaner footage on whatever camera you own. Tie in grading RAW footage in DaVinci Resolve.

### 30–60 Second Script
Sony's FX5 just got Netflix approved — but there's a catch most people are going to miss.

Netflix added the FX5 to its approved camera list and put out a guide on how they want it shot. Basically: record internal RAW — Sony calls it X-OCN LT — at ISO 800, with a feature called Dual Gain turned on.

Here's the catch. If you use the camera's Super 35 crop mode, Netflix says that's not recommended for main footage. And the more compressed recording modes? Also not recommended. So the camera being approved doesn't mean every setting on it is.

For context, this camera is around sixty-three hundred dollars Canadian for the body. That's a lot — but it's a lot less than what Netflix-level cameras used to cost.

My take? Most of us aren't shooting Netflix shows. But the lesson still applies to any camera: shoot the best quality your camera allows, nail your exposure, and spend time in the grade. That's what makes footage look cinematic — not a sticker on the box.

## TOPIC 1 — David Fincher's New Movie Is Getting Roasted for "Blatant" AI

### What Happened
David Fincher's new film *The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth* — the Brad Pitt sequel to *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood* — is getting heavy criticism for what people say are obvious generative-AI shots. Around Nov 19, filmmaker and critic Patrick Tomasso said after an early screening it was "the most blatant and egregious AI use I've ever seen in a studio film," pointing to faces changing mid-scene, weird physics and garbled signs. A trailer shot of a horse cart that appears to be in front of the horse went viral. In an LA Times interview, Fincher said he's "not a Luddite" and asked, in effect, why not use a tool that exists. The AI claims are allegations from viewers — nobody has published a confirmed breakdown of which shots used AI.

### Why It Matters
If one of the most detail-obsessed directors alive is using AI in a studio film, AI in professional video is officially mainstream. It also shows audiences *can* spot it — and they punish it when it looks lazy. That's a lesson for every creator using AI in their edits.

### Why It Suits Me
I shoot cinematic video and edit in DaVinci Resolve, where AI tools (Magic Mask, upscaling, relighting) are already part of my workflow. I can speak to where AI helps and where it crosses the line from a working filmmaker's point of view.

### Hook
"One of the most perfectionist directors in Hollywood just got caught — allegedly — using AI, and the internet is not happy."

### Talking Points
- What people are pointing at: shifting faces, odd physics, garbled signs, a horse cart that looks backwards.
- Fincher's response: he's "not a Luddite" — AI exists, so why not use it.
- The real issue isn't *using* AI — it's AI you can *see*. Audiences notice.

### My Angle
I'm not anti-AI — I use AI tools in Resolve every week. But there's a difference between AI that cleans up a shot and AI that replaces craft. The rule I go by: if the viewer can tell, you've failed. Use AI as the invisible assistant, never the visible star. Ask the audience: would this bother you if you paid for a ticket?

### 30–60 Second Script
One of the most perfectionist directors in Hollywood is getting roasted right now — for AI.

David Fincher's new movie, the Brad Pitt sequel to *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood*, is getting called out by critics who saw it early. One filmmaker said it's the most blatant AI use he's ever seen in a studio film — faces changing mid-scene, weird physics, messed-up signs. There's even a trailer shot where the horse cart looks like it's in front of the horse.

Now, to be fair — these are viewers' claims, nobody's confirmed exactly which shots are AI. But Fincher basically said, "I'm not a Luddite. It exists — should we not use it?"

Here's my take as someone who edits video every week. I use AI tools too — masking, cleanup, upscaling. That's fine. The problem is when the audience can *see* it. The moment AI becomes noticeable, it stops being a tool and starts being a shortcut.

So — would AI in a movie bother you if you paid for the ticket? Let me know.

## TOPIC 2 — A Government Privacy Watchdog Just Said: Don't Buy AI Camera Glasses

### What Happened
The Dutch Data Protection Authority (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens) tested six AI camera glasses sold in the Netherlands and published advice on Nov 19 telling consumers not to buy them for general use. On almost every model, the recording light was hard to see or easy to tape over — and most kept recording when covered. In tests, one pair repeatedly said an ambulance was on the way during a simulated emergency when none had been called, and several had security flaws. The regulator also told venues to ban camera glasses through house rules.

### Why It Matters
Anyone filming in public now has a new problem: people are getting more suspicious of cameras, and regulators are drawing lines around consent. Event and street photographers who rely on trust and visible, honest shooting are directly affected by how this debate goes.

### Why It Suits Me
I'm a photographer who shoots events and people — and I also work security, so I see both sides: the person with the camera and the person responsible for protecting a space. That's a unique, credible angle on consent and hidden recording.

### Hook
"A government privacy watchdog just told people: do not buy AI camera glasses — and the reasons are wild."

### Talking Points
- Recording lights could be taped over — and most glasses kept filming anyway.
- One pair told a tester an ambulance was coming during a fake emergency. None was called.
- The regulator wants venues to ban them — which could change the rules for anyone filming events.

### My Angle
As a photographer, my camera is big and obvious — people know when I'm shooting, and they can say no. That's the deal. Glasses that record secretly break that deal, and they make life harder for honest photographers because everyone gets more suspicious of every camera. From my security-job side: if I were managing a venue, I'd want a clear policy on these too. Transparency is the line.

### 30–60 Second Script
A government privacy watchdog just told people: do not buy AI camera glasses.

The Dutch data protection authority tested six pairs of these smart glasses. On almost all of them, the little recording light was hard to see or easy to cover with tape — and most kept recording anyway. So the people around you have no idea they're being filmed.

It gets worse. In one test, the glasses said an ambulance was on the way during a fake emergency. Nobody was called. Some also had security holes that could let someone else get into the camera.

As a photographer, here's why I care. When I shoot an event, my camera is obvious. People see it, they can say no. That's the deal. Secret recording breaks that trust — and makes every photographer look suspicious.

And I work security too, so honestly? I'd want a clear rule on these at any venue.

Would you be okay with someone filming you through their glasses?

## TOPIC 3 — Instagram Will Now Catch People Reposting (and AI-Copying) Your Reels

### What Happened
On Nov 17, Meta brought its "content protection" tool — previously Facebook-only — to Instagram. Once a creator enrolls, new original Reels are protected automatically, and when someone reposts them, the creator can block the copy, add an "original by" label linking back, keep monitoring it, or release the claim. Meta is also testing detection of AI-generated videos that reuse a creator's original audio, starting with a small group of creators in the coming weeks. Note: this story is one week old — present it as "in case you missed it," not breaking news.

### Why It Matters
Reposts and AI rip-offs steal views and credit from small creators. This gives creators a real tool to get credit back — but it doesn't pay you from reposts the way YouTube's Content ID can, and the AI detection is still only a test.

### Why It Suits Me
My Instagram is my Pixel With Bliss portfolio — my original photo and video work *is* my business. Protecting it is directly relevant, and it's practical, useful info for other creators.

### Hook
"If someone reposts your Reel — or makes an AI copy of it — Instagram can now flag it for you. Here's how to turn that on."

### Talking Points
- Enroll once, and your new original Reels get protected automatically.
- When a copy shows up you choose: block it, label it "original by" you, monitor it, or let it go.
- Meta is testing catching AI videos that steal your audio — but it's only a small test for now, and there's no payout like YouTube's Content ID.

### My Angle
For small creators, credit is currency. A repost with 1M views and no credit is a client you never got. I'd recommend the "original by" label over blocking in a lot of cases — let the repost work as free advertising that points back to you. But stay realistic: it's a test rollout, and it won't pay you.

### 30–60 Second Script
If someone steals your Reel — or makes an AI copy of it — Instagram can now flag it for you.

Meta just brought its content protection tool from Facebook to Instagram. Once you're enrolled, your new original Reels are protected automatically. When someone reposts one, you get options: block it, slap an "original by" label on it that links back to you, keep watching how it performs, or just let it go.

They're also testing something bigger — catching AI-generated videos that reuse your original audio. But that's only with a small group of creators for now.

Here's my take. For small creators like me, credit is everything. A repost that blows up with no credit is a client you never met. So honestly? Sometimes I'd rather label it than block it — let the repost send people back to my page.

Just know it won't pay you like YouTube's Content ID. Check your activity feed to see if you're eligible.

## TOPIC 1 — The New York Times Just Told Its Photographers: Don't Use Photoshop 2027

### What Happened
On November 24, 2026, it was reported that The New York Times emailed its contributing photographers telling them not to use Photoshop 2027 (version 28.0.0 and later) on assignments. A Times spokesperson confirmed it, saying certain functions in Photoshop 2027 "utilize generative AI in ways that may not be apparent to users of the software." The email also warned that using these features can result in metadata identifying photos as AI-generated. Adobe had not responded publicly at the time of reporting.

### Why It Matters
This is one of the biggest news organizations in the world saying the industry-standard editing app is now too risky for documentary work. As AI features get baked deeper into editing tools, photographers may not always know when an edit counts as "AI" — and the metadata (Content Credentials / C2PA) can label your image as AI-generated anyway. That affects trust, client contracts, contests, and journalism work.

### Why It Suits Me
I'm a working photographer who edits every day and covers real events. Clients and audiences trusting that my photos are real is part of what I'm selling. This is a practical "what's in my workflow" conversation, not just tech news.

### Hook
"The New York Times just told its photographers to stop using the newest version of Photoshop. Here's why."

### Talking Points
- The NYT told contributing photographers to avoid Photoshop 2027 (v28+) on assignments; a spokesperson confirmed it.
- Their concern: some AI features work in ways users may not notice, and can leave metadata flagging the photo as AI-generated.
- The bigger issue isn't "AI bad" — it's that the line between a normal edit and an AI edit is getting blurry, and your file's metadata might say something you didn't intend.

### My Angle
Don't panic and uninstall Photoshop — but know your tools. If you shoot events, weddings, or anything people expect to be "real," you need to know which tools are generative and what your exported files say about them. Transparency is going to become part of being a professional photographer. Ask viewers: should photographers have to disclose AI edits?

## TOPIC 2 — TikTok Can Now Help Find Deepfakes of Your Face (Worldwide)

### What Happened
Around November 23, 2026, TikTok began rolling out its Likeness Detection tool to eligible creators worldwide, after testing it with US creators since July. It's opt-in: you verify your identity through a third-party partner, and TikTok flags uploads that may use your face in AI-generated content, which you can report. TikTok also launched a public AI checker in its Transparency Center (no account needed) that checks whether TikTok's own AI tools made or edited an image/video, and updated "Manage Topics" so users can control how much AI content appears in their For You feed.

### Why It Matters
Anyone who shows their face on camera — talking-head creators especially — is a target for AI impersonation. This gives creators a real tool to find fakes. Note the limits: the public checker reads TikTok's own markers (C2PA credentials and TikTok's VeraDura watermark), so it is not a universal "is this AI?" detector.

### Why It Suits Me
I post talking-head videos on TikTok and I'm learning AI tools. Protecting your face and brand as a creator is directly relevant to me and to my audience of creators.

### Hook
"If you post your face on TikTok, there's a new tool you should probably turn on."

### Talking Points
- Likeness Detection is now rolling out globally to eligible creators; it's opt-in and requires ID verification through a third-party partner (TikTok says it deletes ID documents after).
- It flags videos that may use your likeness in AI-generated content so you can report them.
- There's also a free public AI checker — but it only detects content made with TikTok's own AI tools, not everything.

### My Angle
As AI video gets better, your face IS your brand. Small creators think "nobody would deepfake me" — but scams often target smaller accounts too. Turn on the protections platforms give you, but don't treat the AI checker as a lie detector for the whole internet. Balanced, practical take.

### 30–60 Second Script
If you post your face on TikTok, there's a new tool you should probably turn on.

It's called Likeness Detection, and TikTok just started rolling it out to creators around the world. It was only being tested in the US before.

Here's how it works. It's opt-in. You verify your identity through a third-party partner, and then TikTok looks for videos that might be using your face in AI-generated content. If it finds something, you can report it.

They also launched a free AI checker where anyone can upload a video or image — but, and this is important — it only checks for content made with TikTok's own AI tools. So it's not a magic deepfake detector for everything online.

Honestly, as creators, our face is our brand. I'd rather have the protection on and never need it. Would you turn this on? Let me know.
